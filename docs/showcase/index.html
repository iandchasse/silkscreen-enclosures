    The lever. Your press lands on the crank’s pad from the rod, and the nose meets the plunger from it, so without friction the key needs 0.6 times the switch’s force. With friction in the key’s guide and at the two crowns it is still less than the switch alone.

    The rod. The cranks turn on of PLA filament, cut from the spool. It runs in a rail that hangs from the tub’s skin: closed rings, one each side of every crank, and open seats under the switches. It slides in through a hole in the side wall, and a small printed gate dropped into a slot in that wall stops its end, leaving of end play.

    The hooks. Nothing on the key flexes or rubs. Two rigid hooks stand on its chin side, one at each end, and run in two slots in the chin wall, open to the rim. Face up, or held to read, the key rests on its crank and the crank on its switch, so it sits where the switch puts it, with no free travel before the click. Face down it drops onto both hooks, level, and the first press there travels before the click.

    Retention and the chin. To unhook, a key would have to move away from the chin, and its other face already rides the rod rail, so a key cannot fall out; keys go in, and come out, from the inside. Keep the tub face down from the keys until the board is in: before that nothing stops a key sinking. The chin wall moved outward on the tub and the lid to make room.

    Two noses. The crank comes in two versions that differ only at the nose, by 0.28 mm: crank-T for the SHOU HAN TS365ZJ, which JLC fits on every board it assembles, and crank-M for the APEM MJTP1117. Print the one that matches your board’s switch:

        At rest

        Why not in the tub. A key cut out of the tub’s wall bends from one end, so it has to be free along both long edges. The tub prints face down, and its edge toward the screen is the first layer of it the printer lays, with nothing under it. Rev G gave that edge a 45° roof and Rev H a flat top over 1.4 mm of air, and both strung; a support can’t be got into, or out of, a slot that narrow. Rev I made the key a separate part, which printed without that problem, but a clip small enough for the wall couldn’t hold it. Running the key up to the front face would put that edge on the bed, but the front face is the display side: the key would have to be cut free through the bezel’s rounded edge, next to the screen, and it would be twice as stiff.

        Why the lid works. The lid prints back face down. A key that stands on its back face is laid on the bed along its bottom edge and finishes with its top edge, the last layer, so it is free along both without a layer ever printed over air. Its free end is a straight slot down through the lid’s wall, and behind it a slot runs through the back face; both are vertical as it prints, and the wall’s edge beyond the end slot is chamfered mm at the bed so the squashed first layer can’t close it. Its bottom edge is the lid’s own 2 mm round down to its 45° point, then a 45° to the bed, so from the hinge to the free end it stands on about 0.8 mm of bed, beside a 45° foot along its inside; every layer steps out along the round or in along the 45. Behind the foot the lid is cut back at 45° too, 0.75 mm, opening to 1.2 at the free end, which swings further.

        The key. Two parts of one piece. The bar is the wall’s outer layer below the parting line, flush with the case face, from the lid’s back face up to just under the tub’s rim, so the rim can’t pinch it. It is the flexure: mm thick and 2.8 mm tall, rooted in the lid’s wall, and it bends from there for 6.5–10 mm. For the first mm from the root the round and the foot are notched away: there the key is the plain wall section, and that is where most of the bending happens. From just before the pad to its free end the key rises into the tub’s slot as the block, mm tall in all, so it barely bends. The block carries a 10 mm pad, mm proud, with a 7.1 × 2.4 mm flat face and rounded ends (7 mm on the power key), and a 6.0 × 2.3 mm nub (5.0 wide on the power key) against a 2.88 × 1.50 plunger. At the nub it is the full mm, and that stretch is the hard stop. Over the flexure, the tub’s wall is whole.

        The feel. The nub starts 0.25 mm off its plunger. A press at the pad’s centre takes 2.1–2.3 N; near a pad’s root end it takes more, because the key is a lever about its root. The key bottoms after 0.70 mm, 0.2 mm past the click, as the switch’s own travel runs out, so the case takes an over-press, not the switch. The bar bends to at most 1.1 % there, at the hinge.

        What it costs. The keys ride on the lid, so the gap to each plunger depends on how the lid sits. The tongue has mm per face in its groove, so a gap can run from 0.10 to 0.40 mm, one side opening as the other closes. The stop is the lid’s own edge, behind the key, and moves with it, so the switch always reaches its click and never goes past full travel.

        The stack clicks in

        Put the display, the midplate and the PCBA together and push them into the tub as one: the midplate clicks home at detents, and the stack stays there until the lid goes on.

        Why. Until the lid was on, nothing held the stack: it slid in the tub, and nothing told you it was home. The two bottom screws go through the board beside the ribbons’ fold, so a stack sitting anywhere but home is the one way a screw could reach the glass or a ribbon.

        How. At four places, the midplate’s edge is a spring strip: a 0.7 mm strip of the 1 mm plate, cut free by a mm slot behind it and across its tip, so it bends in the plate’s own plane. Near its tip a bump stands mm past the plate’s edge. The wall is only mm away, so on the way in the bump rides mm into it and the strip bends; then the bump snaps into a pocket cut mm into the wall, and that is the click. It prints like the rest of the midplate: flat, screen side down.

        Where it stops. The pocket’s roof catches the bump when the plate is 0.15–0.55 mm short of home (thicker panels sit closer), so the stack is held within that, and a firm pull still takes it out. The pocket runs on toward the front, from z 1.40 to 3.35, enough for the panel’s tolerance, so once the lid and screws pull everything home the bumps sit free in their pockets and fight nothing: the springs, not the detents, still set where the panel sits.

          Edges and logo

          The edges are rounded enough to print as smooth curves. The site’s wordmark sits in the chin, and its book icon on the back.

          Front edges are mm rounds: nine 0.2 mm layers, so the curve reads smooth rather than stepped, and the first layer starts about a millimetre in from the side, which hides elephant’s foot. Back edges are mm, where your hand holds it. The parting line gets a mm V from a chamfer on each half, which reads as deliberate and hides a tenth of misalignment.
