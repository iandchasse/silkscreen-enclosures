Side keys: part of the lid

Each side button is the lid’s own side wall, cut free on three sides; from just before the pad it rises past the parting line into a slot in the tub. The lid prints back face down, so every key stands on the bed and no part of it is ever laid over air. The 4.26″ has five3.68″ has three.

Why not in the tub. A key cut out of the tub’s wall bends from one end, so it has to be free along both long edges. The tub prints face down, and its edge toward the screen is the first layer of it the printer lays, with nothing under it. Rev G gave that edge a 45° roof and Rev H a flat top over 1.4 mm of air, and both strung; a support can’t be got into, or out of, a slot that narrow. Rev I made the key a separate part, which printed without that problem, but a clip small enough for the wall couldn’t hold it. Running the key up to the front face would put that edge on the bed, but the front face is the display side: the key would have to be cut free through the bezel’s rounded edge, next to the screen, and it would be twice as stiff.

Why the lid works. The lid prints back face down. A key that stands on its back face is laid on the bed along its bottom edge and finishes with its top edge, the last layer, so it is free along both without a layer ever printed over air. Its free end is a straight slot down through the lid’s wall, and behind it a slot runs through the back face; both are vertical as it prints, and the wall’s edge beyond the end slot is chamfered mm at the bed so the squashed first layer can’t close it. Its bottom edge is the lid’s own 2 mm round down to its 45° point, then a 45° to the bed, so from the hinge to the free end it stands on about 0.8 mm of bed, beside a 45° foot along its inside; every layer steps out along the round or in along the 45. Behind the foot the lid is cut back at 45° too, 0.75 mm, opening to 1.2 at the free end, which swings further.

The key. Two parts of one piece. The bar is the wall’s outer layer below the parting line, flush with the case face, from the lid’s back face up to just under the tub’s rim, so the rim can’t pinch it. It is the flexure: mm thick and 2.8 mm tall, rooted in the lid’s wall, and it bends from there for 6.5–10 mm. For the first mm from the root the round and the foot are notched away: there the key is the plain wall section, and that is where most of the bending happens. From just before the pad to its free end the key rises into the tub’s slot as the block, mm tall in all, so it barely bends. The block carries a 10 mm pad, mm proud, with a 7.1 × 2.4 mm flat face and rounded ends (7 mm on the power key), and a 6.0 × 2.3 mm nub (5.0 wide on the power key) against a 2.88 × 1.50 plunger. At the nub it is the full mm, and that stretch is the hard stop. Over the flexure, the tub’s wall is whole.

The feel. The nub starts 0.25 mm off its plunger. A press at the pad’s centre takes 2.1–2.3 N; near a pad’s root end it takes more, because the key is a lever about its root. The key bottoms after 0.70 mm, 0.2 mm past the click, as the switch’s own travel runs out, so the case takes an over-press, not the switch. The bar bends to at most 1.1 % there, at the hinge.

What it costs. The keys ride on the lid, so the gap to each plunger depends on how the lid sits. The tongue has mm per face in its groove, so a gap can run from 0.10 to 0.40 mm, one side opening as the other closes. The stop is the lid’s own edge, behind the key, and moves with it, so the switch always reaches its click and never goes past full travel.
